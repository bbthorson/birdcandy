The challenger's trap is that being better isn't a strategy. You already know
your product is sharper than the leader's. So does a meaningful slice of their
customers. They're still not switching, because switching costs more than the
difference is worth to them.

Every dollar you spend closing that gap on the leader's terms — more features,
more sales, more ads — is a dollar spent on the axis where they have more
dollars. That is the whole shape of the problem.

## The asymmetry

Open data is the one move that costs the leader more than it costs you.

You have less accumulated data, fewer captive users, and less revenue booked
against switching costs. That's normally the weakness. Here it's the reason you
can move: the thing that makes the transition expensive is exactly the thing
you have less of.

Publish on open Lexicons and you've changed what the contest is about. The
leader now has to compete on the experience — which is the axis you were already
winning — and every concession they make toward portability weakens the asset
they've been defending. They can match you, but only by dismantling their own
position. That's asymmetric. Most competitive moves aren't.

## Become the on-ramp

The strongest version of this isn't "we also support the open protocol." It's
becoming the path *out* — and the natural landing place once someone's data is
free to move.

Two things follow from that. First, import has to be genuinely, embarrassingly
good: if data portability is the wedge, the import path is the product. Second,
your export has to be just as good, published just as loudly. It sounds like
unilateral disarmament. It's the credibility that makes the rest of the argument
land, and it costs you almost nothing, because the customers you keep are the
ones you were going to keep anyway.

You're not asking anyone to trust you more than the leader. You're asking them
to need to trust you less.

## Layer by layer

### Identity

Let people bring an identity you didn't issue, and let them leave with it. The
customer who knows they can walk out is a customer who will actually try you —
and the switching cost you decline to build is the switching cost the leader
still has to defend.

> **Story slot — identity.** Want the case where a challenger won by accepting
> the incumbent's users on their own terms rather than requiring conversion.
> *(TK — workshop.)*

### Data storage

If you and the leader read from the same open records, storage stops being a
differentiator for either of you. That's a trade you should take every time:
they're giving up a moat, you're giving up a line item.

> **Story slot — data.** Antiphony is our own worked example — two applications
> we build both run on the same audio Lexicons, so neither can win by owning the
> storage. Needs tightening into a story rather than a claim. *(TK — workshop.)*

### Relay

Read the whole network, not just your slice of it. A challenger who indexes
everything can build product for users who haven't signed up yet — which is the
distribution problem, solved from the wrong end, which is the only end available
to you.

> **Story slot — relay.** *(TK — workshop.)*

### App layer

Where you actually win. Ship faster, be clearer, be more useful. Once the layers
below are shared, that's the entire contest, and it's the contest you wanted.

> **Story slot — app.** *(TK — workshop.)*

## First moves

Publish your Lexicons before you're ready to be judged on them. Make import
work in one click. Say plainly, in public, that customers can leave — and mean
it well enough that it becomes the reason they arrive.
