You are the reason this shift is hard. Not because you are slow — because you
are correct about your own incentives. You built a moat, the moat worked, and
nobody gets promoted for draining it.

So let's not start there. Let's start with the part your strategy deck is
already circling: the moat is draining anyway.

## Where you actually stand

Three forces are pulling at the same wall, and none of them require your
participation.

**Agents don't open apps.** When a customer delegates a task, the agent needs
structured data it can read and transact against. If your data is only reachable
through your interface, you are not the destination — you are the obstacle, and
the agent routes to whoever isn't.

**Regulators are prying.** Portability mandates keep arriving and keep getting
broader. You will export the data eventually. Doing it on a schema you chose is
categorically different from doing it on a schema a regulator chose for you.

**Users have stopped accepting it.** Ownership has moved from an activist
position to a default expectation, and the cohort that holds it is the cohort
you need next.

None of that is a prediction. It's the current run rate on an asset you're
still booking at full value.

## The move

The instinct is to defend. The better trade is to convert.

You have the one thing no upstart can manufacture: enough volume that whatever
schema you publish becomes the schema everyone else implements against. That is
a real, compounding advantage — and unlike lock-in, it doesn't decay when the
regulatory or technical weather changes. It gets stronger every time someone
else adopts it.

Lock-in is an asset you can only spend once, and it's spending itself.
Standard-setting is an asset that appreciates.

The company that defines the Lexicon an industry runs on is in a stronger
position than the company merely holding the rows — because everyone building
against that schema is, structurally, building your distribution.

## Layer by layer

The Atmosphere isn't one decision. It's four, and they can be made
independently and in any order. Here is what each one looks like from where
you're sitting.

### Identity

Your users authenticate against you. In the Atmosphere they authenticate as
themselves — a DID, with a handle rooted in a domain. The version of this you
control is the interesting one: every account verified through *your* domain
carries your institution's attestation across every app in the network, whether
or not the user is in your product that day.

> **Story slot — identity.** The newsroom case: every reporter's handle
> verified through the outlet's own domain, so the byline travels with the
> journalist and the attestation travels with the outlet. Needs a named
> example and real numbers before this ships. *(TK — workshop)*

### Data storage

This is the one that reads as heresy internally: records live in repositories
the user controls, described by open Lexicons, and you read them rather than
hold them hostage. The reframe that actually lands with a board is that you
aren't giving away the data — you're giving away the *storage*, which was
never the moat. It was the bill.

> **Story slot — data.** Best analogue is a standards-body migration where the
> incumbent authored the format and kept the market. *(TK — workshop; needs a
> case that survives scrutiny.)*

### Relay

Aggregation is where you keep leverage without keeping custody. Running relay
infrastructure — or the indexing layer above it — means you still see the whole
firehose, still build the proprietary view, still hold the operational
advantage of scale. You've just stopped charging rent for the exit.

> **Story slot — relay.** *(TK — workshop.)*

### App layer

This is where you were always supposed to be competing, and where your actual
advantages live: brand, trust, support, distribution, twenty years of knowing
what your customers need before they ask. Open the layers underneath and this
is the layer you get to win on.

> **Story slot — app.** *(TK — workshop.)*

## What the first ninety days look like

Not a migration. A wedge.

Pick one record type — the least strategically precious one you have — and
publish it as a Lexicon. Watch who builds against it. Measure whether anything
you feared actually happens. Then decide about the second one with evidence
instead of anxiety.

The cost of this experiment is a quarter. The cost of being late is the
position.
